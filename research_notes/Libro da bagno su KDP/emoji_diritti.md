# Rights to use a "pile of poo" emoji on a KDP book cover (copyright, trademark, licences; EU/Italy and US)

Research notes, 15 Nov 2026. Method note: this session's network blocked direct fetches of many primary pages (openmoji.org, unicode.org, kdp.amazon.com, creativecommons.org, joypixels/jsdelivr, emoji.com, ericgoldman.org, copyrightalliance.org, ipkitten.blogspot.com). The GitHub licence statements were fetched directly and are primary-source quotes. The other findings come from search-result summaries of the cited pages and should be re-checked against the live page before anything is printed. None of this is legal advice.

## 1. Is the Unicode character protected? Are vendor emoji designs (Apple, WhatsApp/Meta, Google, Samsung, Microsoft) copyrighted, and what do their terms allow?

### Takeaway
The Unicode code point U+1F4A9 "PILE OF POO" is an abstract character: an encoding plus a name and a short description. The "idea" of a smiling swirl of poo is not owned by anyone. Each vendor's drawing of it is a separate artwork protected by copyright. Apple and Meta/WhatsApp publish no licence that lets you put their emoji artwork on a printed product you sell. Copying the WhatsApp, Apple, Samsung or other proprietary glyph onto a cover therefore needs the owner's written permission. In practice that permission is not available, so do not do it.

### Cited Findings
- Commentators say emoji images "can be and are protected by copyright," and that Apple has not made licensing options publicly available for Apple Color Emoji — [Lizerbram Law](https://www.lizerbramlaw.com/blog/emoji-protected-copyright/); [Plagiarism Today, "Copyright and Emoji"](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2018/04/10/copyright-and-emojis/)
- Apple Community answers (user forum, not an official Apple statement) say Apple emoji are copyrighted. They say Apple's licensing generally covers content used inside apps, not in marketing or publications. One answer, to a self-publisher, says to contact Apple first — [Apple Community thread 254088141](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/254088141); [Apple Community thread 251161186](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/251161186)
- *Cub Club Investment v. Apple* (US, 2022): the court dismissed a copyright suit over Apple's skin-tone emoji. It held that emoji designs get only "thin" copyright protection, which covers virtually identical copying. This cuts both ways for a cover. A near-exact copy of a vendor glyph is the risky case, while your own independent drawing of the same concept is not infringing — [Eric Goldman, Technology & Marketing Law Blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/02/apply-defeats-copyright-lawsuit-over-emoji-depictions-cub-club-v-apple.htm); [World IP Review](https://www.worldipreview.com/news/apple-s-diverse-emoji-s-infringe-copyright-claims-suit-20198)
- WIPO Magazine: even where an individual emoji qualifies for copyright, "its scope of protection may be quite narrow." It also notes that US courts may apply fair use broadly, so the legal picture is unsettled — [WIPO Magazine, "Emojis and intellectual property law"](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/emojis-and-intellectual-property-law-40430)
- At the time of that commentary, there was "no reported copyright infringement case based on the alleged infringing use of an emoji" in ads — [LexBlog, "Copyright considerations for using emoji in commercial ads"](https://www.lexblog.com/2017/04/19/copyright-considerations-for-using-emoji-in-commercial-ads/)
- Meta's policy on its intellectual property forbids using Meta copyrights or trademarks without Meta's prior written permission. It also treats modifying its brand assets (design or colour) as a violation — [Meta Transparency Center, "Using Meta intellectual property and licences"](https://transparency.meta.com/en-gb/policies/community-standards/meta-intellectual-property/); see also [WhatsApp IP Policy](https://www.whatsapp.com/legal/ip-policy)
- Secondary glossary sources say WhatsApp's emoji designs are proprietary to WhatsApp/Meta and not licensed for use outside the app — [EmojiFYI glossary: WhatsApp Emoji](https://emojifyi.com/glossary/whatsapp-emoji/). Emojipedia notes that WhatsApp uses Apple's native emoji on iOS and its own set on Android and Web. A "WhatsApp poop" screenshot taken on an iPhone is therefore actually Apple artwork — [Emojipedia blog](https://blog.emojipedia.org/whatapps-emoji-adventures-from-non-rgi-flags-to-brand-deals/)
- Google is the exception among the big vendors. Its Noto Emoji images are open-licensed (Apache 2.0), as covered in section 3 — [googlefonts/noto-emoji README](https://github.com/googlefonts/noto-emoji)
- Microsoft has released its Fluent Emoji designs under the MIT licence, as covered in section 3 — [microsoft/fluentui-emoji](https://github.com/microsoft/fluentui-emoji)

### Inferences
- Copyright rules in the EU/Italy work the same way for this question (Italian Law 633/1941 and the EU originality standard: an author's own intellectual creation). A vendor's stylised glyph is a protected drawing, and the bare concept is free to use.
- Risk ranking for a cover:
  1. Pasting the Apple, WhatsApp, Samsung or Microsoft (non-Fluent) poop glyph: high risk. There is no licence, and KDP requires you to hold the rights.
  2. Closely tracing one of those glyphs: still risky, because thin protection still catches near-identical copies.
  3. An original drawing of a poop with a face: low risk.
  4. An open-licence set used per its licence: low risk.
- Samsung's emoji are proprietary like Apple's and Meta's. No public print licence was found, so treat them as "permission required."

## 2. Is "Emoji" trademarked (emoji company GmbH), and does that matter for a poop icon or the word "emoji"?

### Takeaway
Yes. The German company emoji company GmbH (also "Emojico") owns EMOJI word/figurative registrations in many countries, including EU and US registrations. It enforces them aggressively, mainly against merchandise sellers who use "emoji" as a brand. The risk to the author comes from using the word "EMOJI" in the title, series name, subtitle or keywords as a brand-like label. Using a poop picture without the word does not raise that issue. Book titles that use a word descriptively are generally treated differently from trademark use, but avoiding the word removes the problem entirely.

### Cited Findings
- emoji company GmbH says it owns the "Emoji" trademark in more than 100 countries. It reports that a German court held that use of "EMOJI KING" infringed its rights. These are company-sourced claims and unverified — [emoji.com, "emoji company GmbH Enforces Its Trademark Rights"](https://www.emoji.com/emoji-company-gmbh-enforces-its-trademark-rights-2-16/)
- Emoji Company and Mattel litigated over the EMOJI mark. Mattel's US applications for UNO EMOJI and EMOJI STYLE were refused for likelihood of confusion with Emoji Company's EMOJI registration. Emoji Company also sought to stop Mattel using EMOJI STYLE on dolls in Germany — [Frankfurt Kurnit / Mondaq, "The Right to Use Emoji"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/trademark/1045644/the-right-to-use-emoji)
- In the US "Emojico" litigation (many Schedule-A style suits against online sellers), a court found infringement and awarded statutory damages. Commentary notes the tension in also calling the use "descriptive" — [Techdirt, "The Word 'Emoji' Is A Protectable Trademark?"](https://www.techdirt.com/company/emojico/)
- Law360 reports that a company owning several "emoji" marks won a partial victory in an EU opposition against Kikkoman's "emojigrid" mark — [Law360](https://law360.com/articles/2285098). The headline does not name the owner, but it is likely Emojico/emoji company GmbH (unconfirmed).
- At the EUIPO, emoji-style signs used as marks are often refused as non-distinctive. In Case R 2305/2022 (Käselow Holding, 1 June 2023), the Board of Appeal upheld the refusal of the "I love you" hand emoji under Art. 7(1)(b) EUTMR — [Novagraaf](https://www.novagraaf.com/jp/node/2033); [Bird & Bird BrandWrites](https://brandwrites.twobirds.com/post/102it8m/can-emojis-be-trade-marks-thumbs-down-from-euipo-leaves-applicant-with-sad-face); [IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2023/07/emoji-not-trademark-for-euipo-but.html)
- German law: the Federal Patent Court has held that a trademark owner generally cannot forbid use of a registered name as a work title, because the public does not see a title as trademark use. The case concerned celebrity names in class 16 (printed matter) — [Kluwer Trademark Blog](https://trademarkblog.kluweriplaw.com/2019/05/03/celebrity-names-in-class-16/)

### Inferences
- The word "emoji" is the trademark-sensitive element, not the poop pictogram. An emoji-style poop drawing has no registered-mark owner, and a bare poop pictogram would likely be non-distinctive in any case.
- Lowest risk: leave "Emoji" out of the title, subtitle and series name, and do not style it as a logo. Neutral words such as "cacca", "faccina" or "poop" avoid the issue. If "emoji" must appear, use it only descriptively in running copy and never as a brand. Emojico has historically targeted products where EMOJI is used as a brand (merchandise, toys) and Amazon/online sellers.
- Emojico's history of mass Amazon seller suits in the US suggests the practical risk on Amazon is real, even if a book title might win on the merits.

## 3. Licences of open emoji sets (Noto, Twemoji, OpenMoji, Fluent, JoyPixels): commercial print use and attribution

### Takeaway
For a commercial print cover:
- **Fluent Emoji (MIT)** and **Noto Emoji images (Apache 2.0)** are the easiest. Commercial use and modification are allowed, and the only duty is keeping the licence notice.
- **Twemoji (CC BY 4.0)** is fine with a credit line.
- **OpenMoji (CC BY-SA 4.0)** works but brings ShareAlike baggage if the emoji is modified or combined into a new artwork.
- **JoyPixels free** is not usable: its free licence is personal-only and explicitly bans books and printed products. Its paid licence would have to be checked.

### Cited Findings

**Google Noto Emoji**
- README licence section: "Emoji fonts (under the fonts subdirectory) are under the SIL Open Font License, version 1.1"; "Tools and most image resources are under the Apache license, version 2.0"; flag images are public domain or exempt from copyright — [googlefonts/noto-emoji](https://github.com/googlefonts/noto-emoji)
- The PNG/SVG of U+1F4A9 in the repo's image folders therefore falls under Apache 2.0. Apache 2.0 allows commercial use and modification. It requires keeping a copy of the licence and attribution notices and marking modified files — inferred from the licence named at [googlefonts/noto-emoji](https://github.com/googlefonts/noto-emoji); full text in that repo's LICENSE.
- Note that the README says "most" image resources. Check the specific folder being used.

**Twemoji (now maintained as jdecked/twemoji)**
- "Graphics licensed under CC-BY 4.0"; "Code licensed under the MIT License" — [jdecked/twemoji](https://github.com/jdecked/twemoji)
- On attribution: "we consider the guide a bit onerous and as a project, will accept a mention in a project README or an 'About' section or footer on a website." For a book, the equivalent is a credit on the copyright page — [jdecked/twemoji](https://github.com/jdecked/twemoji)

**OpenMoji**
- "OpenMoji graphics are licensed under the Creative Commons Share Alike License 4.0" (CC BY-SA 4.0) — [hfg-gmuend/openmoji](https://github.com/hfg-gmuend/openmoji)
- Suggested attribution: "All emojis designed by OpenMoji – the open-source emoji and icon project. License: CC BY-SA 4.0" (with links to openmoji.org and the licence) — [hfg-gmuend/openmoji](https://github.com/hfg-gmuend/openmoji)
- The README does not explain how far ShareAlike reaches into a larger design. Its FAQ (openmoji.org/faq, FAQ.md) could not be fetched — [hfg-gmuend/openmoji README](https://github.com/hfg-gmuend/openmoji/blob/master/README.md)

**Microsoft Fluent Emoji**
- The repository is labelled "MIT license" (LICENSE file in repo) — [microsoft/fluentui-emoji](https://github.com/microsoft/fluentui-emoji)
- MIT allows commercial use and modification on condition that the copyright and permission notice is included in "copies or substantial portions." For a printed book, putting that notice on the copyright page is the practical way to comply.

**JoyPixels**
- The free licence says uses "shall be for personal use only." Commercial, business and non-profit uses require a separate paid licence — [JoyPixels free licence (Gentoo mirror)](https://mirror.umd.edu/gentoo-portage/licenses/JoyPixels); [calculate-linux mirror](https://calculate-linux.org/packages/licenses/JoyPixels)
- The free licence bans use "in the creation or sale of tangible/physical products such as … books, packaging, print-on-demand items," and bans modifying the artwork (including colour changes) and using it in a logo — [JoyPixels licence mirror](https://mirrors.sjtug.sjtu.edu.cn/gentoo/licenses/JoyPixels)
- JoyPixels' brand deck says the free formats are for non-commercial use only — [JoyPixels brand deck 6.0](https://cdn.joypixels.com/brand/brand-deck-6.0.pdf)
- The licence text in the emoji-toolkit package says that using JoyPixels artwork means agreeing to the free licence, and points to a premium licence — [emoji-toolkit LICENSE.md](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/emoji-toolkit@10.0.0/LICENSE.md) (not fetched directly; from search summary)

### Inferences
- **ShareAlike (OpenMoji) for a cover.** Under CC BY-SA 4.0, ShareAlike attaches to "Adapted Material." If the OpenMoji poop is recoloured, redrawn or merged into a new illustration, that adapted artwork must itself be released under CC BY-SA. Anyone could then reuse the cover illustration. Placing the unmodified emoji next to the title text is more like a collection, and the book text would not be affected. The line is fuzzy for a cover, so avoid OpenMoji unless it is fine for the cover art to become CC BY-SA.
- Every option leaves open the separate question of the word "emoji" (section 2).
- Clean choices, from least friction:
  1. Fluent Emoji (MIT): note on the copyright page.
  2. Noto Emoji (Apache 2.0): note plus licence reference.
  3. Twemoji (CC BY 4.0): credit line.
  4. OpenMoji (CC BY-SA 4.0): credit line, with the cover art possibly becoming BY-SA.
  5. JoyPixels: only with a paid licence whose terms explicitly cover print books.
- Suggested copyright-page lines (Italian edition; adapt for the set used):
  - Fluent: "Icona emoji 'Pile of Poo' da Microsoft Fluent Emoji, © Microsoft Corporation, licenza MIT."
  - Noto: "Emoji da Google Noto Emoji, licenza Apache 2.0 (github.com/googlefonts/noto-emoji)."
  - Twemoji: "Grafica emoji di Twemoji, © Twitter, Inc. e altri contributori, licenza CC BY 4.0 (creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)."
  - OpenMoji: "Emoji di OpenMoji – the open-source emoji and icon project. Licenza CC BY-SA 4.0."
  - Add "modificata dall'autore" when the artwork has been modified (required for CC BY 4.0 and Apache 2.0).

## 4. KDP cover and rights rules; known takedowns or cases over emoji on products

### Takeaway
KDP puts all rights responsibility on the publisher. Its content guidelines expressly cover the title and cover art. You must hold the rights to everything you upload, and that includes not infringing copyright or trademark. KDP can ask for proof of rights and can block or remove a title. No published case was found of a book being taken down for an emoji image. The documented emoji enforcement is trademark action by Emojico, and the one emoji copyright suit (*Cub Club v. Apple*) failed.

### Cited Findings
- KDP content guidelines apply to book content, including the title, cover art and product description. Publishers are responsible for following them — [KDP Help, Content Guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672390)
- KDP requires that you hold the publishing rights to any content you upload for sale. Content must not violate copyright, trademark, privacy, publicity or other rights — [KDP Help, Intellectual property / rights](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672410); [KDP Help](https://kdp.amazon.com/help/topic/G200672400)
- KDP may ask for proof that content is in the public domain — [KDP Help](https://kdp.amazon.com/help/topic/G200672390)
- KDP's AI disclosure requirement covers cover and interior images. Publishers must confirm that AI-generated content follows all guidelines, including intellectual property rights — [KDP Help, Content Guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G200672390) (search summary; page not fetched directly)
- Emojico/emoji company GmbH has a record of trademark actions against online sellers and against Mattel — [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/company/emojico/); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/trademark/1045644/the-right-to-use-emoji)
- *Cub Club v. Apple*: the emoji copyright claim was dismissed and the designs were given thin protection only — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/02/apply-defeats-copyright-lawsuit-over-emoji-depictions-cub-club-v-apple.htm)

### Inferences
- With a proprietary vendor glyph on the cover, the author could not show a licence if KDP asked for one. Takedowns on Amazon are usually triggered by rights-owner complaints (Brand Registry / IP reports), not by proactive checks. The likeliest real-world risk is therefore a complaint from Emojico over the word "emoji," or from Apple or Meta over their artwork.
- If an AI tool is used to draw the icon, KDP's AI-content disclosure applies. Prompting it to reproduce "the Apple/WhatsApp poop emoji" would bring the copying problem back.

## 5. Practical recommendation: draw your own, commission one, or use a licence-safe set; how to attribute

### Takeaway
The safest route is an **original poop-with-face drawing**: one drawn by the author, or commissioned with a written assignment or exclusive licence of rights. It carries no attribution duty, no ShareAlike, and no dependence on a third-party licence. The next best is **Fluent Emoji (MIT)** or **Noto Emoji (Apache 2.0)**, with a notice on the copyright page. Never use the WhatsApp, Apple or Samsung artwork, and keep the word "Emoji" out of the title and branding.

### Cited Findings
- Emoji designs get only thin copyright protection, so an independently drawn poop of the same concept does not infringe as long as it is not a near-identical copy — [Eric Goldman blog on *Cub Club v. Apple*](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/02/apply-defeats-copyright-lawsuit-over-emoji-depictions-cub-club-v-apple.htm); [WIPO Magazine](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/emojis-and-intellectual-property-law-40430)
- Open sets such as Twemoji and Noto are released under licences that allow free commercial use and modification — [Plagiarism Today](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2018/04/10/copyright-and-emojis/); licence texts at [noto-emoji](https://github.com/googlefonts/noto-emoji) and [twemoji](https://github.com/jdecked/twemoji)
- Fluent Emoji: MIT — [microsoft/fluentui-emoji](https://github.com/microsoft/fluentui-emoji)
- JoyPixels free licence bans use in books — [JoyPixels licence mirror](https://mirrors.sjtug.sjtu.edu.cn/gentoo/licenses/JoyPixels)

### Inferences
Decision checklist for the cover:
1. **Draw or commission an original icon.** Use your own shape, eyes, mouth, shading and colours. Do not trace any vendor glyph. If commissioned, get a written cessione dei diritti di utilizzazione economica (Italy) or work-for-hire/assignment (US) covering print, ebook and marketing worldwide. No credit line is required.
2. **If using an open set**, download the asset from the official GitHub repo, save the licence file with the project, and add the notice line on the copyright page (see section 3). If you modify the asset, state that it was modified.
3. **If using a stock site**, check that the licence allows print-on-demand covers and has no print-run cap. Avoid "editorial use only" assets and any stock emoji that reproduces Apple, WhatsApp or Samsung designs. Uploaders sometimes post vendor emoji they don't own.
4. **Wording:** avoid "Emoji" as a title or brand element. "Faccina", "cacca", "popò" or "💩" in the description are safer.
5. **Keep evidence**: licence file, download URL and date, and the artist's contract, in case KDP asks for proof of rights.
